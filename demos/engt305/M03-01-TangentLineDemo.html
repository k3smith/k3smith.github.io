Tangent Line and the Derivative

ENGT 305 · Module 3  |  The slope between two points on a curve is an average rate of change. Slide the second point toward the first and that slope settles on a single value: the derivative.

Drag the dark point along the curve, or focus it and use the arrow keys. Shrink h to bring the second point in.

Key idea: A secant line passes through two points on the curve, P at x and Q at x + h. Its slope, rise over run, is the average rate of change between them. A tangent line touches the curve at P and has the same steepness as the curve there. As h shrinks toward zero, the secant slope approaches the tangent slope. That limiting value is the derivative f′(x), the instantaneous rate of change. The lower plot records the tangent slope at every x, which makes the derivative a function in its own right.

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$
In practice: Whenever one quantity is plotted against another, the slope of the curve is a rate. The slope of position against time is velocity, the slope of a water level against time is how fast a basin is filling, and the slope of a load-deflection curve is stiffness. In MATLAB you can get the derivative as a formula with syms x and diff(f, x), then plot it with fplot. When all you have is measured data there is no formula to differentiate, so you use the secant slope between neighboring points as the estimate, which is exactly what this demo does with a small h. The finite difference demo picks that up.
