Poles

Roots of m s² + c s + k = 0, plotted as s/ωn.

ζ = poles:
Key idea: The damping ratio ζ compares the actual damping c to the critical damping 2√(km), the smallest amount that stops oscillation. The natural frequency ωn = √(k/m) is how fast the system would oscillate with no damping at all. The poles are the roots of the characteristic equation m s² + c s + k = 0: their real part sets how fast the motion dies out, and their imaginary part sets how fast it oscillates.

$$m\,y'' + c\,y' + k\,y = 0$$ $$\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{k\,m}} \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$$ $$s_{1,2} = -\zeta\,\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}$$

The constants C1 and C2 are set by the initial displacement and velocity.

In practice: The same three regimes show up in a vehicle suspension, a building swaying after a gust, a gate on its hinge, and a pressure gauge needle. In MATLAB you can solve the equation numerically with ode45 and find the poles with roots([m c k]). In the capstone (Part F), level-pool routing tracks how the water stored in the detention basin responds to inflow: dS/dt = I − Q. In the simplified case where outflow is proportional to storage, that is a first-order equation: one real pole and no oscillation, which is why the basin's outflow lags behind the inflow and peaks lower instead of ringing. The First-Order Response demo covers that case. The real basin is nonlinear because outflow depends on water depth through the outlet equations, so Part F solves it step by step.
