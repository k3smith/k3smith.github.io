Log-Log Power-Law Fit

ENGT 305 · Module 8  |  Many engineering relationships are power laws, and on ordinary axes they are curves. Plot the same data on log-log axes and they straighten out, which turns a hard fitting problem into a straight-line fit.

Ordinary axes

The data follow a curve. Hard to read an exponent from this.

Log-log axes

The same data fall on a straight line. Its slope is the exponent.

Fitted exponent b (the slope): Fitted multiplier a: R² in log space:
Key idea: A power law is a relationship of the form Q = a hb. Taking the logarithm of both sides gives log Q = log a + b log h, which is the equation of a straight line: log Q plays the part of y, log h the part of x, the exponent b is the slope, and log a is the intercept. So to fit a power law, take logs of both columns of data and fit a straight line by least squares. On log-log axes, where each gridline is a factor of 10, the slope reads directly: if Q rises b decades for every one decade of h, the exponent is b.

$$Q = a\,h^{b} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \log Q = \log a + b\,\log h$$
In practice: In MATLAB: p = polyfit(log(h), log(Q), 1); then b = p(1) and a = exp(p(2)). (MATLAB's log is the natural log, so undo it with exp. Using log10 with 10^p(2) gives the same a and b.) Plot with loglog(h, Q, 'o') to see the line. In the capstone (Part H), this is how the outlet rating, outflow against water depth, gets its equation. The exponent also says something physical: an orifice (a submerged opening) gives an exponent near 0.5, a weir (a crest the water spills over) near 1.5, and a V-notch weir near 2.5, so a fitted exponent far from what the outlet type predicts is worth questioning. For the limitations discussion (Part J): the fit is only supported between the smallest and largest depths that were measured, and a real outlet can switch from weir flow to orifice flow as it submerges, which changes the exponent.
