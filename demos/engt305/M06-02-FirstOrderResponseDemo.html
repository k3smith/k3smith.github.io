First-Order Response: Natural and Forced

ENGT 305 · Module 6  |  Fill a tank, cool a part, charge a capacitor: all three follow the same curve. One number, the time constant, sets how fast, and the response is the sum of a part that fades and a part that stays.

Time constant τ: At the cursor: Progress toward the final value:
Key idea: A first-order system has one place to store something (charge, heat, water), and its rate of change is proportional to how far it still is from where it is headed. The time constant τ sets the pace: after one τ it has covered 63.2% of the remaining distance, after three it is 95% of the way, and after five it has effectively arrived. The solution splits into two parts. The forced response (also called the particular or steady-state solution) is where the input is driving the system. The natural response (the homogeneous or transient solution) is the leftover gap from the initial condition, and it dies away as e−t/τ. Add them and you get the full response.

In practice: The same equation covers a capacitor charging through a resistor (τ = R C), a hot part cooling in air, and a tank or basin whose outflow is proportional to how much it holds. In MATLAB, dsolve gives the formula and ode45 gives the numbers: [t, y] = ode45(@(t, y) (yf - y)/tau, [0 20], y0). In the capstone (Part F), level-pool routing tracks the water stored in the detention basin, dS/dt = I − Q. If outflow were simply proportional to storage (Q = S/τ), the outflow would follow this exact curve after a step in inflow, which is why a basin delays and flattens a flood peak instead of passing it straight through. The real outlet is nonlinear, with outflow depending on water depth, so Part F steps through the same balance numerically.
