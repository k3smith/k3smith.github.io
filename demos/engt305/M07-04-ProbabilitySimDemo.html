Experimental vs Theoretical Probability

Trials: Experimental: Theoretical:
Key idea: The theoretical probability of an event is worked out by reasoning: count the equally likely outcomes that make up the event and divide by the total number of outcomes. The experimental probability is measured: run the experiment many times and divide the number of times the event happened by the number of trials. With few trials the two can be far apart. As the number of trials grows, the experimental value settles toward the theoretical one, a result known as the law of large numbers. For an event built from repeated independent tries, the complement rule is the shortcut: the chance of at least one success is 1 minus the chance of none.

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{outcomes in the event}}{\text{all equally likely outcomes}}$$ $$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none})$$

The typical difference is the standard deviation of the experimental value, \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). It shrinks like one over the square root of n, so 100 times more trials buys one more correct digit.

In practice: Simulating an experiment is often the fastest check on a probability you worked out by hand. In MATLAB, rolls = randi(6, 1, n); rolls a die n times, and mean(rolls == 6) is the experimental probability of a six, since the comparison gives 1 for each success. For two dice: s = randi(6, 1, n) + randi(6, 1, n); mean(s == 7). For an event with probability p each try, rand(1, n) < p marks the successes. The flood example is the same calculation as the return period demo and the capstone (Part G): a storm with a 10% chance each year has a 1 − 0.9¹⁰ chance, about 65%, of occurring at least once in ten years.
