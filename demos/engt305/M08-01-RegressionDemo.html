Least Squares and Overfitting

ENGT 305 · Module 8  |  Fit a polynomial to measured data. Move the points, raise the degree, and see why the curve with the smallest error is not always the one to trust.

Drag a point to move it. Select an empty spot on the plot to add a point (up to 12). With a point focused, the arrow keys move it and Delete removes it.

Degree: SSE: R²:
Key idea: A residual is the vertical gap between a data point and the fitted curve (the red segments). Least squares picks the coefficients that make the sum of the squared residuals (SSE) as small as possible. R² is the fraction of the variation in y that the fit explains: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than a flat line at the mean. Raising the polynomial degree always lowers SSE, but past the real complexity of the data the extra terms start fitting the scatter. That is overfitting, and it shows up as wiggles between the points and wild values when you extrapolate, meaning predict outside the range of the data (the shaded zones).

$$\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2 + \dots + b_d x^d$$ $$\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2 \qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i - \bar{y}\right)^2}$$
In practice: In MATLAB, p = polyfit(x, y, d) returns the least-squares coefficients for degree d and polyval(p, x) evaluates the fit. In the capstone (Part H), the outlet rating relates outflow Q to water depth h by a power law, Q = a hb. Taking the logarithm of both sides turns that into a straight line, log Q = log a + b log h, so a degree 1 fit on the logged data, polyfit(log(h), log(Q), 1), gives the exponent b as the slope. For the limitations discussion (Part J), this demo shows the two things to say about any fitted curve: it can only be trusted inside the range of depths that were measured, and a high R² on the points you fitted does not prove the curve predicts well anywhere else.
