Finite Differences: When Smaller Stops Being Better

ENGT 305 · Module 3  |  A computer estimates a derivative by taking a small step and measuring rise over run. Shrink the step and the estimate improves, up to a point. Then it falls apart.

Key idea: A finite difference estimates a derivative from function values a small step h apart, the same rise-over-run as a secant line. Two things go wrong, in opposite directions. Truncation error is the price of using a finite step instead of the limit: it shrinks as h shrinks. Round-off error comes from subtracting two nearly equal numbers that are each only known to about 16 digits, then dividing by a tiny h: it grows as h shrinks. Measured data behaves the same way, only worse, because measurement noise is far larger than round-off. The total error is smallest somewhere in between, so smaller is not always better.

$$\text{forward: } \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \qquad \text{backward: } \frac{f(x) - f(x-h)}{h}$$ $$\text{central: } \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}$$

Truncation error is proportional to h for the forward and backward formulas and to h² for the central formula, which is why the central line on the error plot falls twice as steeply.

In practice: With data in vectors x and y, diff(y)./diff(x) is the forward difference (one value shorter than the data), and gradient(y, x) uses central differences in the interior, which is the better default. With measured data you do not get to choose h: it is the spacing of your samples, and any noise in y is divided by that small spacing. That is why a derivative computed from field data, such as the rate of rise of a water level, often looks ragged, and why the usual remedy is to smooth the data or fit a curve first and differentiate that. For a formula evaluated in double precision, the best step is roughly 1e-8 for the forward formula and 1e-5 for the central one, not the smallest number you can type.
