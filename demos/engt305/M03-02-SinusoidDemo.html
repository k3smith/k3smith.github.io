Key idea: In y(t) = A sin(ω t + φ), the amplitude A is the peak height above the centerline. The angular frequency ω, in radians per second, sets how fast the wave repeats: one full cycle takes the period T = 2π/ω seconds, and the frequency f = 1/T counts cycles per second (hertz). The phase φ slides the wave along the time axis: a positive phase moves it to the left (earlier) by φ/ω seconds. The derivative of a sine is a cosine with the same period, scaled by ω, so a faster wave has a larger rate of change even at the same amplitude.

In practice: Sinusoids describe anything that cycles: a vibrating floor or bridge deck, alternating voltage, a rotating shaft, the tide at a pier. If y is position, the derivative is velocity, and the lower plot shows why a part vibrating at a high frequency can be moving fast even when it barely travels. In MATLAB: t = linspace(0, 8, 400); then y = A*sin(w*t + phi);. MATLAB's sin expects radians, so a phase given in degrees has to be converted with deg2rad(phi) before it is added to w*t. Mixing degrees and radians is the most common reason a plotted wave lands in the wrong place.
