Running Integral: Area That Accumulates

ENGT 305 · Module 4  |  A rate tells you how fast something is changing. The area under the rate curve tells you how much has built up. Sweep across time and watch one turn into the other.

Drag the dark points to reshape the rate curve. Move the time cursor with the slider, or drag across the lower plot.

Rate now: Area so far: Accumulated total:
Key idea: If the upper curve is a rate (velocity, flow), the area under it up to time x is how much has accumulated (distance, volume). Area below the axis counts as negative. Sweeping x from left to right and recording that running area produces a new function, the running integral, drawn in the lower plot. Its starting height is the initial condition, the amount already there at time zero, which is the constant of integration. The two plots are tied together by the fundamental theorem of calculus: the slope of the lower curve at any time equals the height of the upper curve at that time.

$$F(x) = F(0) + \int_0^x f(t)\,dt$$ $$\frac{dF}{dx} = f(x)$$
In practice: With sampled data in vectors t and f, trapz(t, f) gives the total area and cumtrapz(t, f) gives the running total at every sample, which is the lower curve here. Add the starting value yourself: F = F0 + cumtrapz(t, f). Because this rate curve is made of straight segments, the trapezoid rule is exact for it. In the capstone (Part D), the rate is the inflow hydrograph in m³/s and the running integral is the volume of runoff delivered to the detention basin, so the lower curve's final height is the total volume the storm delivers. That is the starting point for sizing storage, since the basin also releases water through its outlet while it fills. Mind the units: flow in m³/s against time in hours has to be multiplied by 3600 seconds per hour to give m³.
