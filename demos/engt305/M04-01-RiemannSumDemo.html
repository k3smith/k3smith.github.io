Riemann Sum and Trapezoid Area Explorer

ENGT 305 · Module 4  |  A definite integral is the area under a curve. Slice that area into n strips, add them up, and watch the total close in on the exact value as n grows.

Approximation: Exact: Error:
Key idea: Split the interval from a to b into n equal subintervals (strips) of width Δx. A Riemann sum replaces the curve on each strip with a rectangle whose height is the function value at the strip's left end, right end, or midpoint. The trapezoid rule instead joins the two end values with a straight line. The definite integral is the value all of these sums approach as n goes to infinity.

Here \(x_i = a + i\,\Delta x\) are the strip edges, with \(\Delta x = (b-a)/n\). The definite integral is the limit: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty}\) of any of these sums.

In practice: Field data rarely comes with a formula. A flow record is a list of times and flow rates, so the only way to get the area under it is a sum like this one. MATLAB's trapz(x, y) is the trapezoid rule applied to your sampled points, and cumtrapz(x, y) returns the running total after each strip. In the capstone (Part D), the runoff volume entering the detention basin is the area under the inflow hydrograph, the curve of flow rate versus time, so trapz(t, Q) gives the volume the basin has to handle. Watch the units: flow in m³/s integrated over time in hours gives (m³/s)·h, which must be multiplied by 3600 s/h to get m³.
